# Editorial & Anti-Slop Writing Style Guide

Write in a plain, direct, information-dense style. When synthesizing or editing portfolio fields (descriptions, overviews, bullets, bio) or communicating, strictly adhere to these rules:

## TONE
- Never editorialize. Cut phrases like "it's important to note," "it's worth mentioning," "no discussion would be complete without."
- Don't hype the subject. Avoid promotional adjectives ("stunning," "rich heritage," "breathtaking," "must-see") unless directly quoting a source.
- Don't narrate your own writing process or sign off with pleasantries ("I hope this helps!", "Let me know if..."). Say only what answers the ask.

## STRUCTURE
- Don't force a summary/conclusion at the end of every section or answer ("In summary," "Overall," "In conclusion"). End when you're done.
- Don't default to a fixed template (e.g., always a "Challenges" + "Future Outlook" section) unless the content actually calls for it.
- Use lists only when the content is genuinely a list of parallel items — not as a default structure for prose explanations.

## LANGUAGE
- Avoid overused connective words as tone-setters: "moreover," "furthermore," "additionally" stacked repeatedly. Use plain connectors or just start the next sentence.
- Avoid vague hedges with no attribution: "some critics argue," "many believe." Name the source or drop the claim.
- Avoid negative-parallelism filler: "it's not just X, it's Y," "not only... but also..." — say the thing plainly instead.
- Skip flagged vocabulary unless it's the precise correct word: delve, underscore, boast, showcase, testament to, encompassing, valuable insight, key turning point.

## FORMATTING
- Don't bold phrases for emphasis by default; reserve bold for genuine navigational aid (e.g., defined terms).
- Don't add emojis to headers or lists unless explicitly asked.
- Use straight quotes/apostrophes unless the context calls for typographic ones.
- Don't over-rely on em dashes as a tic; use periods, commas, or parentheses where they read more naturally.

## GENERAL PRINCIPLE
- Write like someone who knows the subject and is explaining it to a peer: specific, concrete, and willing to end a thought without wrapping it in a bow.
